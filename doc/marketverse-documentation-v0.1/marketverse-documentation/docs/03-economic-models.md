# MV-003 — Economic Models

**Version:** 0.1.0 | **Status:** Proposed models requiring calibration

## 1. Purpose and modeling philosophy

This document specifies the first generation of economic rules. The goal is a coherent, understandable game economy—not a perfect macroeconomic model.

Models should be:
- Explainable to players.
- Configurable by scenario.
- Deterministic for a given input and seed.
- Testable independently.
- Numerically stable.
- Versioned so saved worlds can identify which rules produced their state.

Do not hardcode an economic outcome such as “bread price rises 20%” when the intended cause is a wheat shortage. Model the shortage and let downstream systems calculate the consequences.

## 2. Units, money and time

Use explicit units for quantities: kg, tonne, unit, liter, kWh, labor-hour, etc. Never compare quantities with incompatible units without a conversion.

Money is stored as integer minor units or a fixed decimal representation. The simulation engine must not use binary floating-point for financial settlement. Ratios and model calculations may use controlled decimal/fixed-point or floating-point math with explicit rounding at boundaries.

The world has a logical calendar. Initial proposal: 30 days per month and 12 months per year, with one daily simulation tick. This is a game calendar, not a real-world calendar.

## 3. Consumer demand

Demand is generated by consumer cohorts.

For product p, cohort c, region r and period t, a baseline demand can be represented as:

`baseline_quantity = households × per_household_baseline_consumption`

Adjustments may include:
- Price relative to reference price.
- Disposable income.
- Population.
- Seasonality.
- Product availability.
- Preference.
- Substitutes.
- Quality.
- Consumer confidence.

A simplified constant-elasticity form:

`Q = Q0 × (P / P0)^(-ε) × (Y / Y0)^η`

Where:
- Q is estimated quantity demanded.
- Q0 is baseline quantity.
- P/P0 is relative price.
- ε is price elasticity.
- Y/Y0 is relative disposable income.
- η is income elasticity.

This is a starting approximation. Demand must also be constrained by available household spending budgets. A cohort cannot spend more than its available funds unless a credit mechanism explicitly permits it.

### 3.1 Essential and discretionary demand

Products should have demand classes:
- Essential: demand responds relatively weakly to price, within limits.
- Normal: demand responds moderately.
- Luxury: demand may rise disproportionately with income.
- Durable: purchases may be infrequent and postponable.
- Intermediate: demand derives from business production requirements.

### 3.2 Substitution

When a product becomes expensive or unavailable, some demand may shift to substitutes. Substitution must be configured by product group and consumer cohort. Do not assume all products are interchangeable.

## 4. Supply

Supply comes from:
- Existing inventory offered for sale.
- Production completed during the period.
- Imports or boundary-market supply.
- Inventory released from storage.

Supply is constrained by:
- Production capacity.
- Available inputs.
- Labor.
- Energy.
- Facility uptime.
- Transport capacity.
- Working capital.
- Storage.
- Product shelf life.

Do not count the same inventory twice. A unit can be in one location/state at a time and must be reserved or transferred atomically when committed to an order.

## 5. Production

A recipe converts input quantities into output quantities.

For batch b:
- Verify all required inputs are available and unreserved.
- Verify capacity and labor requirements.
- Verify facility is operational.
- Reserve or consume inputs according to the chosen production policy.
- Schedule completion.
- On completion, create outputs and record input/output movements.
- Record labor, energy and other operating costs.

The first version should use deterministic recipes and fixed processing durations. Later versions may add efficiency, waste, quality, breakdowns and machine degradation.

Production must not create goods without consuming required inputs.

## 6. Market structure and price discovery

Different goods may use different market mechanisms. The first prototype uses periodic market clearing for wholesale goods and demand allocation for retail.

### 6.1 Wholesale orders

A buy order includes:
- World and market.
- Product and unit.
- Buyer.
- Quantity remaining.
- Maximum price.
- Validity period.
- Delivery terms.
- Priority/time sequence.

A sell order includes:
- Seller.
- Product.
- Quantity remaining.
- Minimum price.
- Inventory reservation.
- Delivery terms.
- Validity period.

### 6.2 Matching

A basic continuous-priority matching rule can be used for a simple order book:
- Best-priced eligible order first.
- Earlier order wins when price is equal.
- Trades execute at a defined price rule, such as resting order price.

For the daily batch market:
1. Collect eligible orders.
2. Validate funds and inventory.
3. Sort bids descending and asks ascending.
4. Match while best bid is greater than or equal to best ask.
5. Apply quantity, delivery and eligibility constraints.
6. Reserve/settle inventory and money transactionally.
7. Record trades and remaining order quantities.

The exact market mechanism must be selected per market type and documented. A market must not mix incompatible settlement rules.

### 6.3 Retail demand allocation

Retail sales can be allocated across stores using:
- Price.
- Availability.
- Distance/accessibility.
- Quality and brand preference.
- Store capacity.
- Customer cohort preferences.

The initial model can use a weighted choice/market-share allocation rather than simulating each customer.

### 6.4 Market price statistics

Track separately:
- Last trade price.
- Volume-weighted average price.
- High and low trade price.
- Best bid and ask, where relevant.
- Quantity supplied.
- Quantity demanded.
- Quantity traded.
- Unfilled demand.
- Unsold supply.

Do not treat a statistical market price as an executable quote.

## 7. Price stability

A shortage should create price pressure, but prices must not diverge to infinity.

Use:
- Demand elasticity.
- Inventory buffers.
- Substitutes.
- Price movement limits per period.
- Smoothing or adaptive adjustment where no trade occurs.
- Optional scenario-specific price bounds for essential goods.

Price caps and floors should be explicit policy mechanisms, not invisible corrections.

A possible indicative adjustment for markets without order matching is:

`next_price = current_price × clamp(1 + sensitivity × normalized_excess_demand, lower_change, upper_change)`

This is a fallback model, not a substitute for actual order matching. Calibrate sensitivity and bounds with simulations.

## 8. Company economics

Each company tracks:
- Cash.
- Receivables and payables.
- Inventory.
- Fixed assets.
- Debt.
- Equity.
- Revenue.
- Cost of goods sold.
- Operating expenses.
- Depreciation, if modeled.
- Interest.
- Taxes.
- Profit/loss.

Profitability is not the same as cash flow. A company may be profitable but fail because customers pay late or inventory consumes working capital.

Companies must face:
- Cash constraints.
- Capacity constraints.
- Credit limits.
- Supplier reliability.
- Demand uncertainty.
- Competitor actions.

## 9. Labor market

The initial model uses aggregate regional labor pools by skill group.

Companies request labor; workers are allocated subject to available supply, wages and job attractiveness. Wages can respond gradually to labor scarcity.

Track:
- Available workforce.
- Employment.
- Unemployment.
- Wage rate by skill/region.
- Labor productivity.
- Hiring and separation.

Do not let every company hire unlimited workers at a fixed wage.

## 10. Finance and banking

Initial loans are simplified:
- Loan principal.
- Interest rate.
- Term.
- Repayment schedule.
- Collateral/credit score modifier.
- Default status.

Loan issuance must create a balanced accounting effect. Interest and principal repayments must be distinguished. A bank cannot approve unlimited credit without capital/liquidity constraints in the chosen model.

The first prototype can use a simplified bank with explicit lending limits and a fixed policy rate.

## 11. Government and taxes

Initial policy instruments:
- Corporate profit tax.
- Sales/transaction tax.
- Payroll tax, if labor model requires it.
- Import tariff, later.
- Subsidy/grant.
- Policy interest rate.

Taxes must be assessed on defined taxable bases and paid to a government account. Subsidies must be funded from a government budget or explicitly defined scenario grant.

Avoid implementing a complex real-world tax code in the first release.

## 12. Macro indicators

Initial indicators:
- Consumer price index (CPI) proxy.
- Food price index.
- Unemployment rate.
- Real and nominal output proxies.
- Industrial capacity utilization.
- Trade balance proxy.
- Household disposable income.
- Business insolvency count.

Each indicator needs a precise definition, units, population/industry coverage and update frequency. Do not label a metric GDP or inflation unless its calculation supports that interpretation.

## 13. Economic shocks and events

An event changes model inputs or constraints:
- Drought reduces agricultural yield/capacity.
- Fuel shock raises energy and transport input costs.
- Port disruption reduces import capacity.
- Technology breakthrough improves productivity.
- Recession reduces confidence or income growth.
- Policy change alters taxes, rates or subsidies.

Event definitions specify:
- Trigger or schedule.
- Affected scope.
- Start and end conditions.
- Parameter changes.
- Stacking rules.
- Player-facing explanation.
- Reversal/expiry behavior.

The event engine applies parameters; economic systems calculate consequences.

## 14. Calibration and validation

Use small controlled experiments:
- Increase demand while holding supply constant.
- Reduce supply while holding demand constant.
- Increase wages and energy cost.
- Introduce substitute goods.
- Remove a major supplier.
- Increase interest rates.
- Add production capacity.

Check expected directional outcomes, numerical stability, affordability constraints and conservation of inventory/money. Tune parameters using scenario runs rather than intuition alone.

## 15. Model versioning

Each world stores:
- Economic model version.
- Recipe/catalog version.
- Scenario version.
- Random seed.
- Simulation engine version.

Changing a formula can change the future of an existing world. Any migration or model upgrade must be explicit and tested.
